# Design judgment

Use this when an interface is technically correct but feels generic, awkward or unresolved. Start with the brief and the actual render; abstract taste advice is insufficient.

## Distinguish three kinds of decision

1. **Constraints:** the explicit brief, truthful content, legibility, platform behavior and functional obligations. A subjective preference cannot cancel these.
2. **Conventions:** familiar patterns that reduce learning cost. Depart when there is a clear benefit and a comprehensible replacement.
3. **Expression:** composition, imagery, type, tone and pacing that make the product recognizable. Evaluate expression against the intended audience and situation rather than a universal style ranking.

“Human” usually means choices responding to specific content: a headline breaking at a meaningful phrase, an image cropped around its subject, a table organized around the decisions people make. Adding noise, imperfections or hand-drawn decoration does not by itself produce human taste.

## Diagnose before changing

Look for a cause behind “ugly”: competing focal points, weak grouping, unsuitable type, thin content, inconsistent density, accidental alignment, repetitive pacing, or lack of visual identity. State a falsifiable observation where possible: “Three equally saturated actions compete with the task's primary action,” rather than “the page lacks premium energy.”

Inspect at three scales. At a distance, identify the dominant shape and reading path. At normal viewing size, assess grouping, readability and the task. Close up, inspect glyphs, icon alignment, borders, state transitions and optical corrections. A beautiful icon cannot compensate for a confused page structure.

## Calibrate expression

Choose where a departure from convention helps: perhaps an unusually cropped product image, a useful live demonstration, or an editorial title treatment. Keep the payment form and destructive actions predictable. Operational software can be memorable through excellent hierarchy, calm density and meticulous feedback without a theatrical hero.

Mechanical equality and perceived equality differ. A circular icon can need slightly different bounds from a square; mixed-script labels may need an optical adjustment after inspecting the real fonts. Make small local corrections, preserve the semantic hit target, and check multiple sizes rather than introducing arbitrary exceptions everywhere.

## Decide and revise

For a consequential choice, record the objective, alternatives, selected approach, benefit, cost and condition for reconsideration. Prefer an explanation such as “Use a single task column because patients compare one appointment at a time; retain a summary on wide screens” over “minimalism is best.”

Run a subtraction pass: remove a decorative layer and compare. Keep it if it contributes identity, meaning or orientation. Run a specificity pass: would the same page work unchanged for a bank, a pottery shop and a medical clinic? If so, investigate the content and visual vocabulary. Do not manufacture novelty when the brief calls for familiar utility.

On an open brief, also notice the look you reach for by habit: warm cream with a serif display and a terracotta accent, near-black with a single acid accent, a purple-to-blue gradient hero, one neutral sans for everything, emoji as section markers, everything centered, identical rounded cards. Each is legitimate when this subject chose it, and a stated user or project direction always wins. As an unexamined default it shows that the subject did not shape the page. Ask what in the subject's own world (its materials, units, documents, terms of art) would have produced a different choice, and keep the habit only if you can say why it belongs here. [Authorship](authorship.md) turns this into a pass over a finished render.

Taste remains a reasoned judgment. Report a measured contrast result as measurement and a stronger sense of balance as an aesthetic judgment; never convert the latter into a fabricated scientific score.
